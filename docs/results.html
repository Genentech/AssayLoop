Reading the columns

    Does it generalise to phenotypes it never saw?

    The main table trains and tests on a chronological split, so every broad phenotype family is represented on both sides of it. A harder question is whether the policy has learned something about acquisition or merely about the phenotypes in its training set. Leave one of AssayBench's five broad phenotypes out of training entirely, then evaluate on it. Supervised training already beats a gene-level kNN baseline, and RL improves on that again.

    Scaling

    What buys performance here is screens, not parameters. Training AssayFormer on 1 through all 1,349 training screens improves both the supervised and the RL policy throughout, with RL above SFT at every scale. The gain concentrates in the first few acquisition rounds — by ten thousand genes sampled, even a random policy has found most of the hits, so there is nothing left to be better at.

    Who actually uses the feedback?

    Run the same ten-round loop twice: once with the previous rounds' hit labels in the prompt, and once with them stripped. The gap is a direct measurement of how much of a method's performance comes from adapting to the experiment rather than from its prior. Every LLM family gains from the feedback and so do AssayFormer and AssayLoop — but the LLMs' gains are small enough that the handoff is worth making.
