What the model learned about genes

BPMF factorises the screen × gene hit matrix into a 10-dimensional gene embedding. The paper shows it as three static panels; here it is one scatter you can recolour. The point the figure makes is a negative one, and it is easier to see by switching between colourings than by comparing three pictures: the space organises by phenotype and co-essentiality, not by physical complex or textbook pathway.

How to read it

  • Coordinates. UMAP with a cosine metric on the K = 10 posterior-mean gene factors, at the same seed as the paper figure, so a gene sits where the figure puts it. The axes have no units and no meaning individually — only proximity does.
  • Clusters are found in the original space, not in the projection. HDBSCAN runs on the 10-dimensional embedding with a minimum cluster size of 150; the projection is only how they are drawn. That is why cluster colour looks clean here: it is not circular.
  • Hit rate is a gene's hits divided by the number of public training screens it appears in. Genes in few screens have a noisy rate, which is what the minimum screens present filter is for.
  • Blank annotations are a category, not missing data. Most genes are in no CORUM family and in no Reactome set of 10–150 genes. Those points are drawn in grey and counted in the legend as unassigned rather than dropped, because dropping them would make the annotation look far more complete than it is.

Which embedding is worth starting from?

The obvious way to initialise gene representations is with something that already knows biology — a language-model embedding of each gene's literature, or a Perturb-seq expression profile. Scored against STRING, CORUM, SIGNOR, and Reactome, those embeddings do recover textbook gene–gene relationships best. They are also the harder ones to train AssayFormer on. Initialising from the historical hit matrix instead, which knows nothing about pathways, works better for picking genes.

And it barely moves afterwards

Comparing gene–gene cosine similarities before and after training, and the rank-biased overlap between each gene's neighbourhood at BPMF, after SFT, and after RL: the BPMF-initialised embedding stays close to where it started. Training is mostly learning how to use the geometry, not rebuilding it.
